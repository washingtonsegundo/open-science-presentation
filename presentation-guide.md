# Presentation guide: 20-minute speaking script

This English script follows the 33-slide revised presentation. It contains 2085 spoken words. Time windows total 20 minutes, including short pauses and slide changes. Rehearse to adjust delivery; discussion is additional.

The pilot and its tools are proposals for co-design. Counts and event dates retained from the supplied presentation are historical slide content, not newly measured results.

## Slide 1: Open by design

**Time:** 00:00 to 00:41 (41 seconds)

Good morning, everyone. My name is Washington Segundo, and I coordinate scientific and technical information at Ibict, the Brazilian Institute of Information in Science and Technology. Thank you for the opportunity to join this discussion.

Today, I would like to show how Brazil's public information infrastructure can contribute to our shared Open Science agenda. My central message is simple: equitable cooperation needs services that we can build, govern, and sustain together.

## Slide 2: The entire research cycle

**Time:** 00:41 to 01:20 (39 seconds)

Open Science changes the whole research cycle. Openness begins when we plan a project, continues through the creation and publication of results, and supports their reuse and evaluation.

The outputs include data, software, methods, and reviews, as well as publications. The goal is to make knowledge accessible, open to examination, and useful to society. To achieve this, we need to consider governance and preservation from the beginning.

## Slide 3: From letters to open ecosystems

**Time:** 01:20 to 01:58 (38 seconds)

This transformation has a long history. Researchers once exchanged private letters. Scientific journals, emerging in sixteen sixty-five, created a public record of research. The Web later expanded the speed and reach of communication.

The Budapest, Bethesda, and Berlin declarations advanced Open Access, followed by the UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science. Digital networks now allow different services to support the recording, review, dissemination, and preservation of research.

## Slide 4: Ibict's public mission

**Time:** 01:58 to 02:35 (37 seconds)

Ibict was founded in nineteen fifty-four and is a research unit of Brazil's Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation. Our public mission is to organize, preserve, and disseminate scientific and technological information.

We have a strong tradition in Open Access and Open Science, supporting repositories, national discovery services, research data, persistent identifiers, and digital preservation. This experience gives us a practical basis for international cooperation.

## Slide 5: Four strategic axes

**Time:** 02:35 to 03:13 (38 seconds)

Our work connects four strategic axes. Information integrity and justice concern authorship, meaning, and equitable access. Information for citizenship supports literacy and participation. Information sovereignty concerns our capacity to govern information and infrastructure. Open Science connects these efforts with research cooperation and public trust.

Together, these axes help us ask an important question: how can information services contribute to social development and serve the public interest?

## Slide 6: Open infrastructures connect research to the SDGs

**Time:** 03:13 to 03:52 (39 seconds)

The Sustainable Development Goals give this cooperation a public purpose. Open knowledge supports education and skills development. Shared infrastructure supports innovation, while inclusive access can help address inequalities. Policy monitoring connects our work with accountable institutions, and international networks support partnerships. These are pathways to investigate, rather than outcomes we can assume. The pilot I will propose later connects these goals with public policies and social technologies.

## Slide 7: A Global South action plan

**Time:** 03:52 to 04:29 (37 seconds)

The cooperation agenda involving the African Union, Brazil, China, and South Africa gives us a shared direction. Its principles include equality, mutual benefit, respect for diversity, universal access, and open collaboration.

The six objectives cover governance, open data, shared infrastructure, scholarly communication, public engagement, and skills development. We should connect these objectives to practical responsibilities and resources, involving technical teams, researchers, funders, and institutional leaders.

## Slide 8: Beyond open access

**Time:** 04:29 to 05:04 (35 seconds)

The ecosystem is broader than access to publications. We need open outputs, open processes, open infrastructures, and open participation. Each supports the others.

Citizen science is a useful example: it means doing research with and for citizens. Communities can contribute questions, knowledge, and evidence. Infrastructure must therefore support dialogue and participation, as well as the storage and discovery of research results.

## Slide 9: Responsible openness

**Time:** 05:04 to 05:41 (37 seconds)

Four frameworks help us balance technical quality with social responsibility. FAIR supports the discovery and reuse of data. CARE emphasizes collective benefit, authority, responsibility, and ethics. TRUST focuses on reliable repositories. DEIA brings diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility into the design of research systems.

The guiding principle is to be as open as possible and as closed as necessary, with appropriate consent and safeguards.

## Slide 10: Inclusive publishing

**Time:** 05:41 to 06:21 (40 seconds)

Publishing models also shape who can participate. Green Open Access uses repositories. Gold provides immediate access through the publisher; when publication charges are involved, these can become a barrier for authors. Diamond removes fees for both readers and authors.

I see Diamond as an important priority for equitable communication. Its sustainability requires durable funding, shared governance, and strong editorial standards. Opening access should expand participation throughout the research community.

## Slide 11: Publish, review, curate: the power of preprints

**Time:** 06:21 to 07:00 (39 seconds)

Preprints allow research to circulate before journal publication, opening opportunities for earlier feedback, collaboration, and reuse. The publish, review, curate model connects early sharing with visible critical review and transparent selection or recommendation. We should link the preprint, reviews, author responses, and subsequent versions. Clear review status matters because availability does not establish validity. This approach also makes reviewing and curation visible as contributions to research quality.

## Slide 12: Informational justice

**Time:** 07:00 to 07:36 (36 seconds)

Openness should also deliver informational justice. We can consider three dimensions: the distribution of access and resources, the recognition of different communities and forms of knowledge, and participation in producing and using information.

These commitments require practical safeguards, including consent, privacy, and respect for collective and Indigenous data rights. Decisions about sharing should consider who benefits, who participates, and who might face harm.

## Slide 13: Human mediation

**Time:** 07:36 to 08:13 (37 seconds)

Implementation depends on people. Researchers need accessible services, practical guidance, and support adapted to their disciplines. Institutions need clear responsibility for curation, privacy, and long-term maintenance.

We also need incentives that recognize data sharing, software, and curation. Training alone will have limited impact if research assessment does not value these contributions. Professional teams and institutional leadership must help turn Open Science principles into everyday practice.

## Slide 14: Brazil's national ecosystem

**Time:** 08:13 to 08:49 (36 seconds)

Brazil's ecosystem connects complementary functions across the research cycle. SciELO, journals, and repositories support publishing. BDTD and Oasisbr enable discovery. BrCris connects research information, while Laguna integrates heterogeneous sources. dARK supports identification and Cariniana supports preservation. These functions work best with shared standards, stable funding, and institutional responsibility. Their combined value provides the foundation for the cooperation tools I will describe shortly.

## Slide 15: Cooperation through networks

**Time:** 08:49 to 09:23 (34 seconds)

Networks make this cooperation possible. Brazil's repository and reproducibility communities support shared practice at the national level. LA Referencia connects regional partners, and COAR supports global cooperation among repositories.

These communities bring together professional knowledge, standards, and practical experience. They give us a strong starting point for joint work and help us learn from solutions developed in different contexts.

## Slide 16: The Laguna architecture

**Time:** 09:23 to 09:55 (32 seconds)

Reading this diagram from the bottom, Laguna collects information from different sources and formats. The information is processed and integrated into a semantic repository. Above that, interfaces and dashboards make it available for analysis and services.

This architecture helps transform separate collections into connected information, and its integration work has supported the development of BrCris.

## Slide 17: A national research information system

**Time:** 09:55 to 10:33 (38 seconds)

BrCris connects information about Brazilian researchers, institutions, projects, and outputs. It helps users explore relationships across the research system and supports research management. The fifteen point nine million publications shown here is the platform count displayed in July twenty twenty-six. The interface illustrates the service. Further interoperability with OpenAlex and OpenCitations is an opportunity to investigate, particularly for linking evidence and improving the context available for assessment.

## Slide 18: Oasisbr

**Time:** 10:33 to 11:08 (35 seconds)

Oasisbr focuses on discovering open research outputs. The figures in this slide show more than six and a half million digital objects, including around one point two million theses and dissertations, aggregated from approximately two thousand sources.

It also connects Brazilian research with wider discovery networks, including LA Referencia and Portugal's RCAAP. This gives institutional collections greater national and international visibility.

## Slide 19: BDTD

**Time:** 11:08 to 11:37 (29 seconds)

BDTD is our Brazilian Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations. It provides national discovery of graduate research, while participating institutions continue to host the full texts.

Its public value includes visibility for specialized knowledge, shared metadata, institutional interoperability, and greater access to the results of investment in postgraduate education.

## Slide 20: dARK and sovereignty

**Time:** 11:37 to 12:15 (38 seconds)

dARK develops persistent identification through a decentralized approach based on the Archival Resource Key standard. The work involves LA Referencia, with support from Invest in Open Infrastructure. Shared governance, funding, and maintenance should be designed from the beginning. Institutions need responsibility for the systems they use. Identification supports traceability, while preservation keeps the resources available. The website shown here provides an entry point for further discussion.

## Slide 21: Information Science and reliable AI

**Time:** 12:15 to 12:53 (38 seconds)

Information Science provides an essential foundation for this work. It helps us organize knowledge, describe its context, and understand where information comes from. Data Science helps us analyze that information, while artificial intelligence supports new forms of discovery and interaction.

These fields work together. The value of an AI application depends heavily on the quality of the information behind it, including its metadata, provenance, and curation.

## Slide 22: Infrastructure for trustworthy AI

**Time:** 12:53 to 13:28 (35 seconds)

This slide shows the connection between repositories and AI. Repositories expose research objects. Metadata and identifiers help connect them. Knowledge graphs represent relationships, and these relationships can support AI applications grounded in research evidence.

Preservation, standards, and governance support every step. This is why investment in information infrastructure is also an investment in our capacity to develop useful and accountable AI.

## Slide 23: Semantic retrieval

**Time:** 13:28 to 14:05 (37 seconds)

Semantic systems help people search by meaning and across languages. Vocabularies and ontologies reduce ambiguity, while multilingual tools can make local-language research more visible.

With retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, a question leads to relevant documents in a collection, which then support a generated answer. The answer still needs checking. Our aim is to make its sources visible, preserve context, and help users examine the evidence.

## Slide 24: Applied AI

**Time:** 14:05 to 14:42 (37 seconds)

Ibict's applications show how organized information can support practical services. ServicesPI and Patente.IA help with patent discovery. RDAPP supports access to public-policy documents, and SAVITS supports monitoring and analysis of social technology policies. These experiences can inform the pilot, although they do not imply that its proposed tools already exist. Running models on public infrastructure also requires skills development, source checking, and human oversight.

## Slide 25: Contributions to the six objectives

**Time:** 14:42 to 15:17 (35 seconds)

Ibict can contribute across the action plan's six objectives. Networks and standards support governance. Discovery services support data access, while integration and preservation provide shared infrastructure. Repositories and journals support communication. Citizen science and information literacy support engagement. Education, training, and technology exchange develop skills. Partners can translate this experience into reusable services and clearly assigned contributions to joint work.

## Slide 26: From national delivery to cooperation

**Time:** 15:17 to 15:54 (37 seconds)

International delivery needs clear roles, shared standards, and stable resources. Existing networks give us a starting point. Any additional coordination mechanism should have a defined purpose and added value. A focused pilot lets partners test arrangements, identify barriers, and improve their collaboration. For this presentation, the proposed focus is public policy monitoring and fair research assessment. These strands share a need for connected, trustworthy evidence.

## Slide 27: A joint pilot for policy monitoring and fair research assessment

**Time:** 15:54 to 16:34 (40 seconds)

I propose a joint pilot with two connected strands. The first would monitor public policies, initially focusing on social technologies developed with communities to address public needs. The second would develop tools for fairer research assessment by connecting repositories, journals, and research information systems. Partners would define the questions, responsibilities, resources, safeguards, and timetable together. The aim is to understand how research contributes to society and make that contribution visible.

## Slide 28: Connected evidence can support policy and assessment tools

**Time:** 16:34 to 17:13 (39 seconds)

The pilot could produce three practical components. Links between research outputs would connect publications, data, software, and reviews. A policy dashboard would organize implementation evidence and reveal gaps. Contextual portfolios would bring this evidence together with community accounts for qualitative assessment. We would begin with an agreed sample and document permissions, provenance, and uncertainty. These are proposed tools to test with users, building on existing services and expertise.

## Slide 29: CoARA provides a reference for fairer research assessment

**Time:** 17:13 to 17:51 (38 seconds)

CoARA, the Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment, offers a reference for this work. Its commitments recognize diverse contributions and prioritize qualitative judgement, with peer review supported by responsible indicators. Our proposed tools should help assessors examine evidence and context. They should also be tested for bias and usefulness. Social contribution is one relevant dimension, alongside other research contributions. This proposal draws on CoARA without implying institutional membership.

## Slide 30: What would success look like?

**Time:** 17:51 to 18:31 (40 seconds)

Success should be assessed through documented use and experience. Did policy evidence inform a decision? Did assessment recognize contributions that were previously overlooked? Can communities describe how research was used and what changed? Can partners maintain the tools and apply the skills they developed? We would agree a baseline and review criteria before starting. These observations can support an account of contribution, while causal claims require a suitable evaluation design.

## Slide 31: An offer of cooperation

**Time:** 18:31 to 19:06 (35 seconds)

Brazil can contribute public services, technical experience, and regional partnerships to this agenda. We can connect services, launch the pilot, develop skills, and share evidence. By bringing policy monitoring and research assessment together, we can make the social uses of knowledge more visible. Trust, inclusion, sovereignty, and sustainability should guide both the technical work and the way partners make decisions together.

## Slide 32: Repository communities in Salvador

**Time:** 19:06 to 19:39 (33 seconds)

I would also like to invite you to Salvador, Brazil, in June twenty twenty-seven. Open Repositories will take place from June sixth to tenth, followed by the Brazilian Digital Repositories Network meeting on June tenth and eleventh.

These meetings offer an opportunity to strengthen partnerships, exchange practical experience, and review progress on work that we begin together.

## Slide 33: Thank you

**Time:** 19:39 to 20:00 (21 seconds)

Thank you very much for your attention. The languages on this slide reflect the diversity that our cooperation should welcome. I look forward to your questions and to continuing this conversation through practical partnerships. Obrigado.
